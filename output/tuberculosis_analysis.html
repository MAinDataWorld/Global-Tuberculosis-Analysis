Global Tuberculosis Analysis

A Global, Regional and Country-Level Assessment

1. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the major global public health challenges, contributing to substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide.

Despite significant progress in TB prevention, diagnosis and treatment, the burden of the disease continues to vary considerably across countries and geographical regions.

This project provides a data-driven assessment of global tuberculosis incidence and mortality trends using estimates published by the World Health Organization (WHO).

The analysis examines global patterns, compares tuberculosis burden across WHO regions, identifies countries with the highest estimated incidence and mortality rates, and investigates Egypt’s position within the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR).

Particular attention is given to changes in TB incidence and mortality over time and to comparing Egypt’s epidemiological trends with those observed across the rest of the Eastern Mediterranean Region.

1.1 Project Objectives

The main objectives of this analysis are:

  • Examine global trends in tuberculosis incidence and mortality.

  • Compare TB incidence and mortality rates across WHO geographical regions.

  • Identify countries with the highest estimated tuberculosis incidence and mortality rates.

  • Determine Egypt’s global ranking in terms of TB incidence and mortality.

  • Examine tuberculosis burden across Eastern Mediterranean countries.

  • Assess Egypt’s regional ranking in TB incidence and mortality.

  • Compare historical TB trends in Egypt with population-weighted estimates for the rest of the Eastern Mediterranean Region.

2. Data and Methodology

2.1 Data Sources

This analysis uses data obtained from the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Programme.

Two source files were used:

  1. TB Burden Dataset: Contains country-level estimates of tuberculosis incidence, mortality, population and related epidemiological indicators.

  2. WHO TB Data Dictionary: Provides definitions and reference information for the variables included in the original dataset.

The datasets are stored in the data/raw/ directory of this project.

2.2 Data Preparation

The original WHO tuberculosis dataset was imported into R using the readr package.

Variable names were renamed to improve readability and consistency throughout the analysis.

The dataset was subsequently restricted to the variables required for the analysis, including country identifiers, WHO regional codes, reporting years, population estimates, TB incidence rates and TB mortality rates.

The WHO data dictionary was used to extract regional codes and their corresponding geographical names.

These regional names were then joined to the main dataset to facilitate regional comparisons.

2.3 Analytical Methodology

Population-weighted averages were calculated to estimate global and regional tuberculosis incidence and mortality rates over time.

Latest available country-level and regional estimates were extracted to facilitate comparisons of the most recent reported disease burden.

Countries were ranked according to their estimated TB incidence and mortality rates.

Percentage changes in regional incidence and mortality rates were calculated to assess changes between the first and last available years.

Finally, Egypt’s TB burden was examined separately and compared with population-weighted estimates for the Eastern Mediterranean Region excluding Egypt.

9. Conclusion

This analysis demonstrates a substantial decline in tuberculosis (TB) incidence and mortality in Egypt between 2000 and 2024. The estimated incidence rate decreased from approximately 21 to 10 cases per 100,000 population, while the mortality rate fell from approximately 1.5 to 0.4 deaths per 100,000 population. These reductions indicate considerable progress in reducing the national TB burden over the study period.

In 2024, Egypt ranked 151st globally in TB incidence and 162nd in mortality. Within the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR), Egypt ranked 16th out of 22 countries for incidence and mortality, according to the minimum-rank method used in this analysis. This relatively favourable position is consistent with the comparison showing that Egypt’s incidence and mortality rates remained substantially lower than the corresponding estimates for the rest of the EMR throughout the study period.

Although TB incidence and mortality declined globally and across most WHO regions, the magnitude of progress varied considerably. The EMR continued to experience a higher TB burden than Egypt, highlighting differences in progress between countries. Despite Egypt’s improvements, the persistence of TB cases and deaths, particularly in recent years, underlines the importance of maintaining effective control measures. Continued efforts in early detection, timely treatment, surveillance, and access to healthcare are essential to sustain the progress achieved and further reduce the burden of TB.

Overall, the findings place Egypt among the relatively lower-burden countries globally and within the EMR, while demonstrating a clear long-term improvement in both TB incidence and mortality. As the analysis is based on WHO estimates, the results should be interpreted as trends in estimated disease burden rather than direct measures of the impact of specific interventions.
